## What This Document Is

These are the gold standard. Every piece of generated content should feel this good. If it doesn't, the prompts need tuning.

Use these examples to:
- **Benchmark** generated content against (does it feel as good as these?)
- **Test** the review bots (all 4 should pass every example here)
- **Seed** the staging database so the iOS app has content to display during development
- **Train your eye** — after reading these, you'll know instantly when output is off

Each example includes the full content item (headline, talking points, body) plus annotations explaining WHY it works.

## Example 1: News — Transfer Confirmed (Arsenal)

**Type:** `news`
**Team:** Arsenal
**Newsworthiness Score:** 9
**Emotional Context:** `exciting`

### Headline

> Big news, Arsenal just signed a new striker and your boyfriend is probably losing his mind right now.

**Why it works:**
- Doesn't start with "Arsenal" (boring)
- Immediately connects to her partner ("your boyfriend")
- Creates curiosity (who did they sign?)
- Feels like a friend texting, not a news alert
- 104 characters — well under 200 limit

### Talking Points

1. "So Arsenal signed this guy called Viktor Gyokeres from Sporting Lisbon. He scored like 40 goals last season. If he brings it up, just say 'That's a massive signing' and watch his face light up."

2. "He cost around £85 million, which is a LOT. If you want to wind him up a bit, say 'Was he really worth that much?' Guaranteed debate starter."

3. "The reason this is big: Arsenal have been struggling to score in big games. This guy is supposed to fix that. So if he seems over the moon, that's why."

4. "If his mates are texting about it too, you could casually drop 'I heard about the Gyokeres signing' into the group chat. Hero status."

**Why they work:**
- Each one is a script she can actually use
- They're ordered by usefulness: basic reaction → banter → context → power move
- Football info is embedded naturally inside conversation advice
- Point 2 shows it's okay to be playful and tease
- Point 4 gives her a social scenario beyond just talking to her partner

### Body

> Arsenal just made their biggest signing in years. Viktor Gyokeres, a Swedish striker who's been absolutely tearing it up in Portugal, is officially joining the club for around £85 million.
>
> To put that in perspective, that's one of the most expensive transfers in Arsenal's history. He scored 43 goals last season at Sporting Lisbon, which is genuinely ridiculous. For context, most strikers are happy with 15-20.
>
> Why it matters: Arsenal have been solid defensively (they're good at stopping the other team from scoring) but have been missing a proper goalscorer in the big games, the ones against the top teams that decide the title. Gyokeres is supposed to be that missing piece.
>
> Your boyfriend's mood tonight: expect excitement levels somewhere between "kid at Christmas" and "proposing to the TV screen." This is the kind of signing fans dream about. Let him have his moment.

**Why it works:**
- "Tearing it up in Portugal" — casual phrasing, not "performing exceptionally at Sporting CP"
- Explains the fee in context ("one of the most expensive")
- The stats comparison ("most strikers are happy with 15-20") gives her a frame of reference
- "Defensively" is explained immediately in parentheses
- Ends with a partner mood prediction — the most useful part for her
- 4 paragraphs, scannable in ~45 seconds

## Example 2: Matchday — Big Rivalry (Arsenal vs Tottenham)

**Type:** `matchday`
**Team:** Arsenal
**Newsworthiness Score:** N/A (matchday is always sent)
**Emotional Context:** `exciting`
**Rivalry Level:** `derby`
**Pre-Match Mood:** `nervous`

### Headline

> Derby day. Arsenal vs Tottenham tonight and honestly, don't be surprised if he can't eat dinner.

**Why it works:**
- "Derby day" signals importance immediately
- "Can't eat dinner" is relatable and funny — she'll recognise this behaviour
- Doesn't explain what a derby is yet (that's for the body) — just sets the tone
- 96 characters

### Talking Points

1. "This is THE rivalry. Arsenal and Tottenham are both from North London and they genuinely despise each other. Think of it like two siblings who've been competing since birth. Bring it up and he'll have opinions."

2. "If you want to seem like you're paying attention, ask him 'How do you think Saka's going to do tonight?' Saka is Arsenal's star player and this is the kind of game where he usually turns up."

3. "Fun fact you can casually drop: Arsenal haven't lost to Spurs at home in over 3 years. If he's nervous, remind him of that. Instant brownie points."

4. "The game kicks off at 17:30. If he goes quiet about an hour before, that's normal. It's not about you. He's mentally preparing."

**Why they work:**
- Point 1 explains the rivalry in human terms (siblings, not "historical North London fixture")
- Point 2 gives her a SPECIFIC thing to say with a SPECIFIC player name
- Point 3 is a power move — she knows a stat he might not have thought about
- Point 4 is pure emotional intelligence — managing her expectations about his behaviour

### Body

> It's North London Derby day, which is basically the Super Bowl of Arsenal's season (okay not quite, but emotionally it's up there). Arsenal are hosting Tottenham at the Emirates tonight at 17:30, and your boyfriend has probably been thinking about this all week.
>
> Here's the deal: Arsenal and Tottenham are from the same part of London and they've been rivals for over a century. It's not just about football, it's about bragging rights. If Arsenal win, he gets to gloat to any Spurs-supporting mates. If they lose, he will be inconsolable. There is no middle ground.
>
> Good news: Arsenal are in great form. They've won 4 of their last 5 games, they're sitting 2nd in the league, and they haven't lost to Spurs at home since 2022. Saka has been their best player this season with 12 goals, and big games are where he shines.
>
> The worry? Tottenham have actually been decent lately too. They've won their last 3 away games, and Son Heung-min (their captain, you might recognise him from those supermarket ads) always seems to score against Arsenal.
>
> Tonight's atmosphere at home: expect pacing, shouting at the TV, and absolutely zero attention directed at anything else for about two hours. This is normal. Bring snacks.

**Why it works:**
- Opens with "Super Bowl" comparison — instant understanding of importance
- "Your boyfriend has probably been thinking about this all week" — relatable
- Explains bragging rights in friend-group terms
- Son Heung-min is introduced with a real-world reference ("supermarket ads") — not just "Tottenham's forward"
- Closing paragraph is practical relationship advice, not football info
- 5 paragraphs, scannable in ~55 seconds

### Post-Match Cheat Sheet

> **If they win:** "That was massive, right?! You must be buzzing." (Keep it simple. Let him do the talking.)

> **If they lose:** "Unlucky. They'll bounce back though." (Do NOT say 'it's just a game.' Trust me.)

> **Bold prediction:** "2-1 Arsenal" (Safe, optimistic, shows you have an opinion.)

**Why it works:**
- Win response is enthusiastic but brief — she's mirroring his energy
- Loss response includes a WARNING about what NOT to say — this is gold
- The prediction gives her something to throw out before kickoff that makes her seem invested

## Example 3: News — Transfer Rumour (Manchester United)

**Type:** `news`
**Team:** Manchester United
**Newsworthiness Score:** 7
**Emotional Context:** `drama`

### Headline

> Transfer gossip alert. There's a rumour Man United are trying to sign a midfielder from Barcelona. He'll definitely bring this up.

**Why it works:**
- "Gossip alert" sets the tone — fun, not serious
- "He'll definitely bring this up" gives her a reason to care
- 134 characters

### Talking Points

1. "The rumour is that Man United want Frenkie de Jong from Barcelona. This has been going on for YEARS, it's like a will-they-won't-they romance. If he mentions it, just roll your eyes and say 'Not this again.' He'll find it hilarious."

2. "If he seems excited about it, ask 'Do you actually think it'll happen this time?' It's the right question because fans have been burned before. He'll appreciate that you get it."

3. "Quick context: Man United's midfield has been their weak spot. Getting de Jong would be like finally hiring a competent manager after years of chaos at work. Big upgrade."

4. "Fair warning: transfer rumours can drag on for WEEKS. If he keeps refreshing Twitter and mumbling about 'reliable sources,' this is why."

**Why they work:**
- Point 1 frames a transfer saga as a rom-com — she gets it instantly
- "Not this again" is a genuinely funny thing to say that shows she's been paying attention
- Point 3 uses a workplace analogy
- Point 4 prepares her for the behavioural side-effect (Twitter refreshing)

### Body

> Every transfer window, Man United fans go through the same cycle: hope, excitement, rumour overload, and then usually disappointment. We might be entering that cycle again.
>
> Multiple sources are reporting that Man United are in talks to sign Frenkie de Jong from Barcelona. If that name sounds familiar, it should. This exact rumour has popped up basically every summer since 2022. It's the transfer that never quite happens.
>
> De Jong is a Dutch midfielder who's really good at controlling the game (think of the person at work who keeps everything running smoothly, that's what he does on the pitch). Man United have been desperate for someone like him because their midfield has been... let's say underwhelming.
>
> The catch: Barcelona are reportedly asking for £75 million, and de Jong seems quite happy in Spain. So this could go either way. Your boyfriend will have strong opinions on whether it'll happen. Ask him. It's a guaranteed 20-minute conversation.

**Why it works:**
- Opens by setting expectations about the transfer window cycle — this prepares her
- "This exact rumour has popped up basically every summer" — she's now in on the joke
- De Jong's ability explained via workplace analogy
- "Guaranteed 20-minute conversation" — she knows exactly what she's getting into
- Honest about uncertainty ("could go either way") rather than overselling

## Example 4: News — Injury to Key Player (West Ham)

**Type:** `news`
**Team:** West Ham
**Newsworthiness Score:** 7
**Emotional Context:** `bad_news`

### Headline

> Heads up, Jarrod Bowen got injured in training today. He might be a bit gutted tonight.

**Why it works:**
- "Heads up" frames it as practical info for her evening
- Focuses on the PARTNER'S reaction, not just the football fact
- "A bit gutted" is understated British — matches the audience
- 86 characters

### Talking Points

1. "Jarrod Bowen is basically West Ham's best player. He scores the most goals and creates the most chances. So this is a big deal, kind of like a band's lead singer pulling out of a tour."

2. "It's a hamstring injury (back of the thigh) and he could be out for 3-4 weeks. If he seems down about it, just say 'How long is Bowen out for?' Shows you know it matters."

3. "The silver lining you can offer: 'At least it's not a long-term thing, right?' This is genuinely helpful because hamstring injuries CAN be worse. You'll sound informed and reassuring."

4. "West Ham play Liverpool this weekend without him. If he's worried about that, he's right to be. Liverpool are top of the league. Maybe don't mention that bit though."

**Why they work:**
- Point 1 uses the band analogy — she instantly understands the impact
- Point 2 tells her exactly what to say, with the physical explanation in plain English
- Point 3 gives her a genuinely useful reassurance line — she's being supportive, not just informed
- Point 4 is funny — the "maybe don't mention that bit" shows the app has emotional intelligence

### Body

> Not great news for West Ham today. Jarrod Bowen, who's been their most important player this season, picked up a hamstring injury during training and is expected to miss 3-4 weeks.
>
> To understand why this matters: Bowen has scored 11 goals this season and created more chances than anyone else in the team. Without him, West Ham lose their biggest attacking threat. It's like removing the engine from a car. Technically it still looks like a car, but it's not going anywhere fast.
>
> The timing is particularly rough because West Ham play Liverpool this Saturday. Liverpool are currently top of the league and are in incredible form. Without Bowen, that's gone from "tough game" to "we might get battered."
>
> For you: he might be in a bad mood about this, especially as the weekend approaches. The best thing you can do is acknowledge it. "Gutted about Bowen" goes a long way. And resist the urge to say "it's only football." We cannot stress this enough: never say "it's only football."

**Why it works:**
- The car analogy is simple and effective
- Honest about the Liverpool game being bad — she shouldn't sugarcoat it to him either
- Final paragraph is pure relationship advice — the most valuable part
- "We cannot stress this enough" breaking the fourth wall creates intimacy with the user
- Ends with the most important lesson, not the football facts

## Example 5: News — Low-Key Funny Story (Manchester United)

**Type:** `news`
**Team:** Manchester United
**Newsworthiness Score:** 6
**Emotional Context:** `funny`

### Headline

> This is more gossip than news, but Man United's manager just said something in a press conference that fans are losing it over.

**Why it works:**
- "More gossip than news" sets the right expectations
- "Fans are losing it over" creates curiosity
- She can decide whether to tap through or not — no pressure
- 128 characters

### Talking Points

1. "The manager basically said he's 'not happy with the attitude of some players,' which in football speak means there's DRAMA behind the scenes. If he's talking about it, just say 'That sounded intense' and let him vent."

2. "This kind of thing usually means one of two things: either a player is about to get dropped (benched), or someone's getting sold. Ask him 'Who do you think he's talking about?' He'll have a theory."

3. "Fun angle: football press conferences are basically like reality TV. The managers drop hints, journalists try to catch them out, and fans read into every single word. He's probably been analysing the body language all afternoon."

**Why they work:**
- Point 1 translates manager speak into real-world meaning
- Point 2 gives her a question that makes HIM do the talking — low effort for her, high engagement for him
- Point 3 reframes press conferences as entertainment — she might actually find this funny

### Body

> Man United's manager held a press conference today ahead of this weekend's game, and he did that thing managers do where they say something that sounds calm but is actually a grenade.
>
> When asked about the team's recent performances, he said he's "not happy with the attitude of certain individuals" and that "changes will be made." In normal life, this would be like your boss sending an all-staff email saying "we need to have a conversation about standards." Everyone panics.
>
> Fans are now going through the squad player by player trying to figure out who he's talking about. Twitter is a mess. Fantasy football teams are being reshuffled. It's chaos, and it's exactly the kind of chaos football fans secretly love.
>
> If he brings this up tonight (and he probably will) you don't need to have an opinion. Just ask "Who do you think he's on about?" and then sit back. You've just bought yourself 30 minutes of him talking passionately about squad dynamics while you nod supportively.

**Why it works:**
- "Sounds calm but is actually a grenade" — she gets the drama immediately
- The all-staff email analogy is perfect — every working adult has experienced this
- "Twitter is a mess" acknowledges the fan culture without making fun of it
- Last paragraph: practical, funny, and self-aware about what the app is really for

## Anti-Pattern Examples (What NOT to Generate)

These are examples of bad content that the review bots should catch.

### Bad Example 1: Sounds Like BBC Sport

> **Headline:** "Arsenal confirm the signing of Viktor Gyokeres from Sporting CP for a reported fee of £85 million."

**Why it's bad:**
- Reads like a news wire
- No personality, no connection to the partner
- Uses the formal club name "Sporting CP"
- Zero conversation value

**Tone Bot should catch:** Fail — reads like sports journalism, not a friend texting

### Bad Example 2: Condescending

> **Headline:** "Football can be confusing, but here's something cool, Arsenal signed a new player!"

**Why it's bad:**
- "Football can be confusing" — she knows that, don't remind her
- "Here's something cool" — patronising, like talking to a child
- Exclamation mark feels forced

**Tone Bot should catch:** Fail — condescending framing

### Bad Example 3: Too Much Jargon

> **Headline:** "Arsenal's new signing could fix their issues in the final third and improve their xG output significantly."

**Why it's bad:**
- "Final third" — unexplained football term
- "xG output" — might as well be in a different language
- "Significantly" — dry, academic tone

**Tone Bot should catch:** Fail — unexplained jargon
**Brevity Bot should catch:** Fail — not scannable for target audience

### Bad Example 4: Factually Wrong

> **Headline:** "Saka scored twice last weekend and Arsenal are now top of the league!"

**(When Saka actually scored once and Arsenal are 2nd)**

**Why it's bad:**
- Wrong goal count
- Wrong league position
- If she repeats either of these to her partner, she loses credibility

**Accuracy Bot should catch:** Fail — two factual errors (goal count, league position)

### Bad Example 5: Way Too Long

> **Talking Point:** "Arsenal are playing Tottenham this weekend, which is a really important game because they're both from North London and have been rivals for a long time, dating back over 100 years, and the fans from both sides take this game very seriously because it's about local pride and bragging rights, so if your boyfriend seems stressed or excited this week, that's probably why, and you might want to give him some space on matchday because these games can be emotionally intense for fans."

**Why it's bad:**
- One enormous sentence
- Covers too much ground — should be split into 3 separate talking points
- Exhausting to read
- Nobody would text like this

**Brevity Bot should catch:** Fail — talking point exceeds 2 sentences by a mile

### Bad Example 6: Not Newsworthy (Spam)

> **Headline:** "Just a reminder that Arsenal play next Tuesday! Thought you'd want to know."

**Why it's bad:**
- Not news, not interesting, not useful
- "Just a reminder" is filler
- Fixture reminders are not what the app is for — the matchday briefing handles this
- This is exactly the kind of notification that makes someone delete an app

**Newsworthiness score should be:** 2 (routine/boring) — should never reach the review bots

## Using These Examples

### For Development
1. Seed the staging database with these 5 examples (2 Arsenal, 2 Man United, 1 West Ham)
2. Use them to verify the iOS feed and detail view render correctly
3. Test that all card types (news, matchday) display properly
4. Verify talking points formatting and body markdown rendering

### For Prompt Tuning
1. After running the pipeline on real data, compare generated content to these examples
2. Ask: "Would this example feel at home next to the golden examples?"
3. If no — identify what's different and adjust the prompts
4. Re-run and compare again

### For Review Bot Testing
1. Feed each golden example through all 4 review bots — they should all pass
2. Feed each anti-pattern through all 4 review bots — at least 1 should fail
3. If a golden example fails review, the review bot prompt is too strict
4. If an anti-pattern passes review, the review bot prompt is too lenient
